Which linear-attention mixer should we ship?

Run 1 results and independent audit · run_019fe0f9-1bbd-702c-b141-6d58e128bda6 · 6 operators × 3 random seeds = 18 training runs · 390M-parameter model, 599,785,472 tokens each · 8×A100-40GB · measured 2026-08-08, audited 2026-08-08 · Revision 2

The short version

Ship KDA as we already have it (KDA_BASE), or the variant with the convolution activation removed (KDA_NOACT). The two are indistinguishable in this data; either is defensible.

The decision rests on speed and memory, not on quality. The quality measurement came back inconclusive — the experiment was not sensitive enough to tell these six operators apart on quality, and we knew before running it that this was a likely outcome. Speed and memory, by contrast, separate cleanly and are not in doubt.

Do not ship the Householder operators (KDA_R1, KDA_R2) — they are 22–30% slower for no measured quality gain. But see §7: that cost turns out to be a fixable implementation problem, not a property of the idea, and the idea itself is available to us for free through a different route.

1 · What this was for, and the vocabulary you need

The decision. We are about to spend real money training a large production model. Inside a transformer, every layer has a component that lets tokens exchange information — call it the mixer. Standard attention is one choice; it is accurate but its cost grows with the square of sequence length. Linear-attention mixers keep a fixed-size running summary instead, so cost grows linearly and generation is cheap. Several competing designs exist. This experiment was run to pick one.

Terms used throughout:

  • Cross-entropy (CE) — how surprised the model is by text it has never seen. Lower is better. Measured in nats. Our models land around 3.05 nats; the differences we are hunting are in the third decimal place (0.01–0.03 nats).
  • Throughput — training tokens processed per second. Higher is better. Directly proportional to how much the run costs.
  • Peak / reserved memory — GPU memory used. Peak is what the model actually holds; reserved is what it asks the allocator for. Reserved is the number that decides whether a bigger model fits on the card.
  • Seed — the random number that sets the starting weights and the data order. The same design trained with three different seeds gives three slightly different results. That spread is our measurement noise.
  • "Not resolved" — the difference between two designs was smaller than our noise, so we cannot say which is better. This is not the same as saying they are equal.

How the comparison was kept fair. All six versions are byte-identical except for the mixer placed in two specific layers (slots 6 and 11) out of sixteen. Everything else — the six standard-attention layers at positions 2, 5, 8, 10, 12, 14, all the feed-forward blocks, the vocabulary, the learning-rate schedule, the data — is the same. So a difference between two versions is caused by the mixer and nothing else.

Two details that were forced on us, and matter later:

  • The six standard-attention layers could not be removed. Position information (RoPE) is only attached to real attention layers in our codebase. An arm with fewer attention layers would silently lose its sense of word order, and its worse score would look like a mixer effect when it was actually missing position. A previous project lost a result to exactly this.
  • Bigger mixers were paid for by shrinking the feed-forward layers, so every version has the same parameter count to within ±0.05%. Otherwise a "better" mixer might just be a bigger model. This matters in §6 — it is the source of one of the errors we found.

Each version was trained three times (three seeds), which is the minimum that lets you estimate noise at all. As §5 explains, three turned out not to be enough for quality.

4 · The gated convolution: why the answer depends on what you compare it to

This is the most instructive result in the study, and it is a lesson about experimental design rather than about the mechanism.

Compared against the shipped reference, the gated convolution looks better by 0.0062 nats. Compared against the no-activation control, the same arm looks worse by 0.0051 nats. Same arm, opposite sign.

The reason is exact algebra. The gate our mechanism applies, 2·sigmoid(a·u)·u, is identically equal to (2/a)·silu(a·u) — a SiLU activation with a learnable slope. (Verified to 8.9×10⁻¹⁶, i.e. floating-point exact.) So switching the gate on does not just add a gate: it also relocates and reparameterises an activation function that was already there. Comparing against the reference moves three things at once, and no part of that difference can be attributed to any one of them.

The honest comparison is against the control, where the only remaining difference is the gate itself: +0.0051 nats, 95% CI [−0.031, +0.041], p = 0.77. That interval spans the entire range of effects we care about, in both directions. The correct statement is "this experiment cannot tell the gate apart from no gate" — not "the gate is worse," and not "the gate is fine." Without the control arm, this study would have produced a plausible, publishable, wrong number.

5 · Why quality came back inconclusive, and why that is the honest answer

Across the 18 runs, the seed-to-seed noise in CE is σ̂ = 0.0204 nats. With three seeds per arm and six arms compared properly, the smallest difference this design could reliably have detected is 0.0636 nats.

The differences we are looking for, from published work on these operator families, are 0.010 to 0.030 nats. That is 2–6× smaller than what we could see. The experiment was outmatched by the question.

The formal results all agree: overall test F(5,12) = 2.49, p = 0.091 — not significant. No individual comparison against the reference excludes zero once corrected for making five comparisons at once.

Please do not read the quality ranking in §3 as a ranking. The arms are listed in CE order because the table has to be in some order. The gap between best and worst (0.0484 nats) is almost exactly the width of a single confidence interval (0.0484 nats). Any of the six could be genuinely best.

This outcome was written down in advance. The pre-registration filed before the run states that the design might not resolve CE differences and that the recommendation would then fall back to speed and memory. So this is a planned branch, not a failure — and the fallback axes did separate cleanly.

Two things make the quality numbers upper bounds, not estimates.

  • The training budget is small. Each model saw 1.5 tokens per parameter. Published work clusters at 3–20, and our own production model sits at 27–44. Architecture differences measured at low budgets systematically overstate: in our own earlier measurements, one such gap halved (0.0103 → 0.0059 nats) when the budget grew 15×. So every CE difference here should be read as a ceiling on what it would be at production scale.
  • A null result is a bound, not a proof of equality. We did not pre-declare an equivalence margin, so we are not licensed to claim any two arms are the same — only that any difference is smaller than roughly 0.048 nats.

The useful by-product: σ̂ = 0.0204 nats is the first real measurement of noise at this scale in this project. It replaces a 5.5×-wide guess with a factor-2.3 bracket, and any future experiment should be sized from it.

6 · What the independent audit changed — read this before quoting revision 1

After the results were written up, twelve independent reviews re-derived every number from the raw per-run data, the analysis code, the model source, and the AWS logs. The core statistics all reproduced exactly (σ̂, the overall test, the correction factor, the detection limit — all confirmed to five significant figures, and cross-checked against scipy). The primary quality numbers were also confirmed bit-for-bit against the original training logs.

But six specific claims in revision 1 were wrong. All six were in the hand-written summary, not in the automatically generated analysis, which was clean throughout. They are corrected here.

Three housekeeping defects, none of which move a conclusion

  • The machine-readable results.json in the results folder is a stale snapshot taken mid-run. It describes 8 of 18 runs, lists three arms as "NO DATA," and reports the gated-conv comparison with the opposite sign. Anyone parsing it instead of reading this document gets a materially wrong answer. It must be regenerated before this branch is merged.
  • 16 of the 18 per-run result files were transcribed by hand, not captured programmatically. Detected by an exact arithmetic identity that must hold in binary (per-device throughput × 8 = total) and fails in 13 of 18 files. The important fields are unaffected — quality and both memory figures are full precision in all 18 and match the original logs exactly; the rounding on throughput is at most 0.026%, against arm differences of 2–29%. But the files should be re-extracted from the logs.
  • A guard was silently disabled by its own configuration. The analysis script keeps a list of "fields we have accounted for" so it can warn about fields nobody checked. Five fields — including the broken MFU and FLOP figures — were on that list but never actually read anywhere in the 3,200-line script. The warning therefore came back empty, and the generated report skips from section 5 to section 7 because section 6 had nothing to print. The check built to catch exactly this bug was switched off by declaring the bug's fields as checked.

7 · The most useful finding: the reflection mechanism is available to us for free

The 22% slowdown we measured is not the price of the mechanism. It is the price of our own unfinished kernel — and we do not need that kernel.

"Negative eigenvalues" sounds like a deep change to the mathematics. In implementation it is one multiplication: a gate value β computed as sigmoid(x) becomes 2·sigmoid(x). That is the whole mechanism. It is a change to how one number is produced, not to the kernel that consumes it.

Concretely, in our codebase and in the third-party library we already depend on:

  • The optimised library kernel chunk_kda already accepts this option as a documented public argument, implemented as a single scale factor. No GPU kernel in the library references it at all — the fast code is simply unaware of β's range.
  • There is no range check, no clamp, and no upper bound on β anywhere in that code path. The matrix the algorithm inverts has ones on its diagonal and zeros above, so its determinant is exactly 1 for any value of β — the widening cannot make it singular.
  • Our shipped KimiDeltaAttention already contains the two lines, behind a config flag it already exposes. Our GatedDeltaNet has been training with the wide range on the fast kernel by default, for the entire project.
  • The paper that introduced the idea states it outright: "for DeltaNet, implementing the extended eigenvalue range is straightforward, since there is no need to modify the Triton kernel."
  • Stability was already measured in our own tree: the wide-β regime reached β = 1.93 and landed in the same numerical-accuracy band as the baseline, with all test cells finite.

So the fix is one configuration field, not an engineering project. Our hand-written Householder kernel, whose own documentation says it is a sequential implementation "expected to be materially slower" and built for "mechanism validation, not throughput," was never needed to get this mechanism at R=1.

Why our kernel is slow — diagnosed

It walks the sequence one position at a time: 4,096 dependent iterations, three times per training step. It uses no tensor cores at all, and runs at about 3.7% occupancy — achieving 0.22% of the GPU's arithmetic peak. It is limited by latency and serialisation, not by the amount of arithmetic. The optimised library kernel instead processes 64 positions at a time.

The extra reserved memory has a single identified cause: the backward pass keeps a full-precision copy of the running state for every position, ~2 GiB at our shape. Good news — it is freed per layer, so it does not grow with the number of layers using the operator. It does grow with batch size and sequence length.

But be careful what you claim it buys

In this run, the wide-β arm scored +0.0236 nats — worse than the reference, and well inside the noise. Two published sources also report no consistent language-modelling gain from this change.

What it demonstrably buys is state tracking — the ability to represent algorithmic patterns a contracting update cannot. In the source paper, a parity task goes from 0.233 to 0.982 accuracy with this one change.

So the correct pitch is free optionality, not free quality. Turning it on costs nothing measurable, and makes a capability available. It is not a quality upgrade, and should not be sold as one.

Note this only covers R=1. Getting two or more reflections per token at full speed still needs kernel work — see the box below.

If we later want more than one reflection per token (R > 1), start with a measurement

A plan was on record to make the R>1 case fast by re-expressing it on top of an existing, already-optimised library kernel (chunk_dplr_delta_rule). An independent review of that plan found the idea sound but three of its details wrong, and it changes what an engineer should do first:

  • The risk we were most worried about is not real. We feared the library's backward pass would be numerically unstable under the padding trick the plan needs. There are zero divisions anywhere in that kernel's source, and the padding pattern is already what the library itself ships for this exact case. A different, milder risk was found instead: one intermediate matrix becomes ill-conditioned precisely when β is large — worth watching, not blocking.
  • The memory saving was overstated. The claim "1.00 GiB → 32 MiB" is the R=1 number. At R=2 it is closer to 64–176 MiB. Still a real 6–17× improvement, so the memory argument survives — but do not quote 32 MiB. (Our own kernel's source also overstates its own workspace by 1.5×, on the numbers used to size this decision.)
  • It might not actually be faster. The replacement kernel builds four internal matrices where the simpler one builds one, works in 16-position chunks rather than 64, and recomputes its forward pass during the backward. It could land slower than the 0.705× it is meant to fix.

Therefore: day one is a half-day throughput benchmark, not a week of implementation. That single measurement either justifies the work or saves it. And do not modify the other candidate kernel (chunk_gated_delta_product) — its backward pass is shared with the production Gated DeltaNet path, so patching it would put our best-throughput arm at risk.

8 · A warning about reusing the speed numbers

The treatment operator ran in only 2 of 16 layers. Everything else was identical, so the entire slowdown came from those two layers. Divide it out and the operator itself is running roughly 5× to 21× slower than the shipped one — not 22% slower. The whole-model figure is diluted by the fact that most of the model was untouched.

This means the penalty grows if we use the operator in more layers, which is the usual direction for these architectures:

Layers using the Householder operator2 of 16 (measured) 4 of 168 of 1614 of 16

Do not quote 0.779× or 0.705× for any design that uses a different number of linear-attention layers. The model reproduces the measured value exactly at 2-of-16, which is what makes the extrapolation trustworthy. Memory does not scale this way and is not the blocker — time is.

What being wrong would cost

If we pick the worse operator and its true quality deficit is 0.010–0.030 nats, the fix is simply to train on a few percent more tokens. On a 1B-parameter production run (27–44B tokens, roughly $1,000–1,650) that is:

The arithmetic that settles it.

The entire quality ambiguity is worth $77–484 on the production run. Removing it costs $2,900–11,300. Meanwhile the speed choice — which is already resolved to about 1% — carries a larger production penalty ($286–687) than the quality question does.

So: do not buy more seeds. Do not buy more tokens. Training longer is plausibly worse on both axes at once, because these architecture differences shrink with budget faster than the noise does, while cost rises in proportion.

What is worth buying

  1. The one-field experiment, ~$55–65. Three runs on the same frozen seeds, with the reflection range turned on via the fast kernel. This gets the mechanism at full speed and tests whether the reproducibility problem was our kernel. Gate it on two receipts: a logged β above 1.0 (proving the flag took effect) and throughput parity with the reference.
  2. A recall-focused re-scoring, ~$79–171 plus 2–4 engineer-days. All 18 final checkpoints are still in S3, so no retraining is needed. Aggregate quality averages over mostly-easy tokens; the published evidence is that these operators differ on recall, where one study found a 20-point gap at near-identical perplexity. This is the only option that returns a new kind of evidence. Not free: the mask-builder script does not exist yet, and the scoring path is single-rank and will hang on 8 GPUs until fixed.
  3. Optional, ~$220–250: a focused two-arm reference-vs-GDN2 comparison, if that result needs formally closing rather than just correctly describing.

10 · What we did not measure — the honest gaps

  • Nothing about inference. We measured training throughput only. The entire practical argument for linear attention is cheap single-token generation, and that path was never timed. For a production decision this is the largest gap.
  • Only one configuration. One sequence length (4,096), one batch shape, one node, one GPU type. The four fast arms are within 2.1% of each other — close enough that a different shape could re-order them.
  • No recall or long-context measurement. The secondary endpoint designed for exactly this was never wired up (see §9, item 2).
  • No test in our codebase has ever checked kernel accuracy at β above 1 — which is the regime both Householder arms actually ran in. The existing β test recomputes the arithmetic inside the test itself and never calls the kernel. This is a real gap in the shipped result, not only in the proposal.
  • The one test that checks our fused kernel does not read future tokens requires a GPU, and we found no evidence it was run before these 18 jobs launched. On a machine without a GPU it skips silently, which looks identical to passing.
  • The three "data seeds" are not three orderings of the same data. Each run sees only 0.24% of the corpus, so they are ~99.8% different samples. Harmless for comparing arms (all six share a seed's data), but it means our noise figure mixes optimisation noise with data-sampling noise.
